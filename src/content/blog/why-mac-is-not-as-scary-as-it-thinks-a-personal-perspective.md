## There is still a lot of skepticism in IT when the topic of introducing Macs into the organization comes up.

-   Can we manage them properly?
-   Can we secure them?
-   Will they create more support tickets?
-   Can our support team embrace them?
-   Do they fit into our Microsoft environment?
-   And are they not just too expensive?

I understand the skepticism.

Many IT departments have spent years building processes, support models, security requirements, and operational routines around Windows. When Macs enter the conversation, they can quickly feel like something foreign. Something that breaks the model. Something that creates exceptions.

But after working with Mac implementations in larger organizations over the last four years, my experience has been very different.

Mac is not that scary.

That is not just a personal feeling from being a Mac user myself. It is also what I have seen in real enterprise environments when Mac is introduced with the right management, security, onboarding and support model.

In fact, I often see the opposite. Users become happier, more self-sufficient, and more satisfied with their work device. And when users have a tool they actually enjoy working on, it changes the way they work.

## The price is not always the most important argument

Looking at the device in isolation, a MacBook Pro at 25-30,000 DKK(4-5.000 USD) can seem expensive.

And yes, that is a lot of money for a laptop.

But in a business context, that is rarely the full picture.

If an employee already costs the company 50–70.000 DKK per month in salary, and the organization expects that person to be productive, creative, efficient, and deliver high-quality work, then the price of the work device is probably not what breaks the business case.

That does not mean every user needs the most expensive Mac. Of course, hardware should match the actual need.

But if the right device makes an employee more productive, more satisfied, and less dependent on IT support, then it becomes difficult to evaluate the decision based only on the purchase price.

In IT, we talk a lot about cost management. And we should.

But sometimes we become so focused on acquisition cost that we forget the value of better productivity, less friction, and higher employee satisfaction.

## Users often become more self-sufficient

One of the arguments I often hear against Macs is that they will create more support.

That is not necessarily my experience.

When Macs are configured correctly, and users have a stable platform that works well, many of them actually require less help. Not because Mac is magic, but because the experience is often simpler. Hardware and software work well together, and many users feel that the device simply gets less in the way.

I also have a small personal story about this.

Around 15 years ago, my wife switched to Mac. At the time, I was very Windows-focused, so I said to her, slightly coldly:

“Then I will not be able to help you with support anymore.”

She replied:

“I do not think that will be necessary.”

And she was right.

Two years later, I had helped her once with a printer installation. Apart from that, she had been completely self-sufficient.

Not long after, I switched to Mac myself.

And I have not looked back since.

## Mac should not be treated as an exception

One of the biggest mistakes I think organizations make is treating Mac as an exception.

Something that needs to be handled manually.  
Something only for “creative users.”  
Something that does not really fit into enterprise management.

But that picture is becoming more and more outdated.

With Apple silicon and modern versions of macOS, users get an extremely powerful work device. Performance, battery life, stability, and user experience are at a level that is hard to ignore.

At the same time, the management story has improved significantly.

Mac does not need to be a parallel IT track without control. It can absolutely be part of a modern endpoint management strategy.

## Intune can actually do the job

For many Microsoft-based organizations, the question has often been:

“Can we manage Macs without Jamf?”

The short answer is: yes, in many scenarios you can.

Microsoft Intune has become much stronger for Mac management. That does not mean every organization should drop other tools, and it does not mean Intune solves every advanced scenario perfectly.

But for many companies, Intune is now strong enough to manage a modern Mac baseline covering enrollment, compliance, configuration, security policies, certificates, FileVault, Microsoft Defender, and app deployment.

And with Platform SSO, Mac is starting to feel much more natural in a Microsoft 365 and Entra ID environment.

That is an important shift.

Because when users get a good sign-in experience, and IT can manage the device through Intune, Mac is no longer something the organization has to “tolerate.” It becomes a real, supportable enterprise platform.

And perhaps most importantly:

Intune does not make the Mac worse.

That is probably one of the biggest concerns from Mac users. That IT will come in and “Windows-ify” their Mac. That management will make the experience slower, heavier, and more restricted.

But when done correctly, that is not the experience.

On the contrary, proper management can make onboarding, security, and operations easier without destroying what makes Mac a great work device in the first place.

## IT needs to challenge its own skepticism

I am not saying Mac is the right choice for every user.

There will still be applications, business scenarios, financial considerations, operational requirements, and support models that need to be evaluated seriously. This should not become a religious Windows versus Mac discussion.

But if you are sitting as a classic Windows hardware user, IT administrator, or decision-maker and you cannot see Mac as a relevant tool in your organization at all, then I think you are wrong.

Not because Windows is bad.

But because Mac today is a strong enterprise platform. And because the employee experience matters more than we sometimes acknowledge in IT.

We talk a lot about productivity, adoption, and the digital workplace.

But sometimes it starts with something very simple:

Does the employee have a work device they actually want to use?

If the answer is yes, the impact can be bigger than we think.

Mac is no longer just a request from a few individual users. It is a serious work device that can be managed, secured, and integrated into modern Microsoft environments.

So maybe the question is no longer:

“Can we allow Macs in the organization?”

Maybe the better question is:

“Why are we still so afraid of them?”
